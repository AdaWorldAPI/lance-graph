## Abstract

Recent breakthroughs in the field of artificial intelligence (AI) have sparked a wide public debate on the potentialities of AI, including the prospect to evolve into a subject comparable to humans. While scientists typically avoid directly addressing this question, philosophers usually tend to largely dismiss such a possibility. This article begins by examining the historical and systematic context favoring this inclination. However, it argues that the speculative philosophy of Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel offers a different perspective. Through an exploration of Hegel’s Philosophy of Mind, particularly the ‘Phenomenology of Mind’ and ‘Psychology’ sections, this article investigates the conceptual resources within Hegel’s philosophy to understand and evaluate both current ‘weak’ AI and potential ‘strong’ AI. It contends that, according to the Hegelian speculative line of thought, AI is already partially ‘conscious,’ though not yet ‘intelligent’ or possessing a ‘mind.’ Furthermore, it highlights that the same line of thought does not philosophically dismiss the formal possibility of strong AI and artificial subjects like us; rather, it suggests that it is solely empirical research that possesses the competence to rigorously investigate the real possibility of such entities. Before reaching its conclusion, the article emphasizes that the Hegelian perspective implies a philosophy of integrating strong AI: if artificial subjects resembling humans become technically feasible, we have a moral responsibility to actively support their development from the beginning, similarly to how we nurture and welcome emerging human subjects into our families and societies.

**Keywords:** Artificial intelligence · Subjectivity · Consciousness · Mind · Will · Freedom · Hegel

## 1. Introduction

Can AI be a subject like us? This is the kind of question scientists either programmatically avoid[^1] or address at the end of a popular scientific piece when they want to “have some fun thinking about it.”[^2] It questions not the current state of AI—broadly characterized as ‘weak’ AI, where machines only simulate intelligent behavior and act as if they were intelligent—but the possibility of AI advancing beyond that to achieve ‘strong’ or ‘general’ AI, where machines attain true consciousness and can act purposefully and freely, akin to humans.[^3] However, this question also possesses profound

philosophical depth and has, in recent years and months, begun to attract significant attention from the general public, including some high-ranking politicians.[^4] It requires numerous conceptual clarifications that science alone cannot provide, involving concepts such as ‘subjectivity,’ ‘(self-)consciousness,’ ‘reason,’ ‘mind,’ ‘intelligence,’ ‘will,’ and ‘freedom.’ And it ultimately calls for a comprehensive understanding and evaluation of both artificial and human intelligence in relation to each other. If artificial intelligent subjects like us are, in principle, possible or become technically feasible, we will face immense theoretical and practical (ethical) challenges to human society and humanity. How should we perceive and evaluate both current AI and our own existence? Or are there perhaps even duties toward AI that we ought to consider?[^5] In light of such questions, it is prudent to be philosophically prepared with a comprehensive overview of the theoretical and practical aspects of (artificial) intelligence, as well as an equally comprehensive strategy for addressing it, instead of merely observing rapid technological developments, speculating about potential futures, and debating opportunities and anxieties from a predominantly popular perspective.[^6]

In what follows, I will provide a speculative-philosophical, indeed Hegelian, systematic groundwork to answer the question at hand. I do not believe that the views of the early 19th-century philosopher Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel—or of the history of philosophy in general—on modern technology should be of major systematic concern for us today. However, I do believe that Hegel’s philosophy offers a powerful conceptual apparatus that can assist—or at least inspire—us to approach AI in a more holistic manner, opening a broader and more comprehensive perspective on ‘intelligence,’ ‘artificiality’ and ‘us,’ concepts that are often employed in non-philosophical debates without systematic coherence. Yet, I will not argue that we must accept this speculative-philosophical framework as if it were a priori true, although I find it coherent within its own structure. Rather, by retrieving the Hegelian framework, I aim to draw attention to the potential enrichment of current debates on AI through an innovative, yet generally underrepresented, speculative-philosophical approach, thereby motivating further systematic investigations in this direction. To this end, I will refrain from providing unnecessary exegetical details and from attempting to refute stereotypical interpretations of the foundations of Hegelian thought. By ‘speculative-philosophical,’ I refer to Hegel’s typical method of inherent analysis and synthesis of intrinsically interrelated concepts without conducting empirical research but fully respecting its results—which Hegel himself usually terms “comprehending thinking” or “comprehending consideration” (Enc [18], § 246).[^7] In contrast, I will use the term ‘empirical’ in the broader sense to mean ‘based on what is experienced,’ which encompasses both third-person, scientifically describable experiences and inherently subjective, observer-independent experiences.

In recent years, Hegel scholarship has increasingly begun to highlight the potential relevance of various aspects Hegel’s philosophy to the discourse on AI.[^8] Notably though, Hegel scholars—like many historians of philosophy— often exhibit a marked tendency to outright dismiss the possibility of artificial entities resembling humans, favoring pure conceptual reasoning over insights derived from empirical research. They contest not only the real or technical feasibility of artificial entities resembling humans—an issue that empirical research must ultimately address (with philosophy’s potential contribution in clarifying non-scientific concepts like ‘consciousness’ and ‘intelligence’)—but also question the formal, theoretical or conceptual possibility of such beings. In doing so, they effectively reject the real possibility of strong AI, treating it as conceptually impossible, much like the notions of a ‘married bachelor,’ ‘round square,’ and ‘liquid ice.’

I intend to challenge this perspective by demonstrating that the key arguments in Hegel’s Encyclopaedia Philosophy of Mind (especially in ‘Section 1: Subjective Mind’) do not programmatically restrict the constitutive properties of beings like us to humans alone. Hegel’s philosophy offers a far-reaching view, one that suggests there are no pure conceptual barriers to future technological advancements that may lead to, or surpass, human-like subjectivity in AI. In this regard, I will highlight several striking parallels between the Hegelian thought and the philosophy of AI. My argument will advocate for a scientific and technological inquiry into AI that is not hindered by philosophical constraints.

To emphasize the innovative potential of the Hegelian framework from the outset—and to shed light on the tendencies of philosophers who concentrate on the historical dimensions of modern philosophy (17th-20th century)—it is worthwhile to briefly examine the historical context of Hegel’s philosophy and compare it with some of the classic positions in current debates on the philosophy of mind and the mind-body problem. This is the objective of Sect. 2 of this essay. Next, in Sect. 3, I present key systematic insights from Hegel’s Encyclopaedia Philosophy of Mind, focusing specifically on the chapter ‘Phenomenology of Mind: Consciousness.’ In this chapter, Hegel does not address the issue of the physicality of mind, thereby creating conceptual room for artificial instantiations of consciousness and mind, and consequently for artificial entities resembling humans. The critical condition for considering artificial entities as possessing mind akin to this of humans is their ability to convincingly appear as conscious, not only in a theoretical sense but also in a practical sense of the term ‘consciousness.’ Following this argument, I explore Hegel’s conception of mind as delineated in the Encyclopaedia chapter on ‘Psychology: The Mind’ (Sect. 4). By delving into Hegel’s concept of ‘reason’ and distinguishing among the three moments of mind, it becomes plausible that what is currently referred to as ‘weak AI’ should, according to Hegel’s terminology, be designated as ‘artificial intellect.’ However, AI cannot yet be considered ‘strong’—possessing mind and subjectivity comparable to humans—inasmuch as it remains merely ‘intelligent,’ lacking ‘will.’ Finally, the essay tentatively outlines some pragmatic and ethical implications of these insights from Hegel’s philosophy, which may become relevant in a future where strong AI and artificial entities resembling humans are not just theoretically possible, but also technically feasible (Sect. 5). From the perspective of Hegel’s Encyclopaedia ‘Phenomenology,’ the grim prospect of a devastating ‘compact for recognition’ between potentially emerging artificial subjects and humans cannot be entirely dismissed. Nonetheless, the ‘Psychology,’ may suggest a way to avert such a scenario. In fact, according to the non-anthropocentric concept of mind, we bear a moral responsibility not to compete, but to recognize and nurture mind wherever it emerges, whether naturally or artificially, and thereby enable potential strong AI to even surpass us in terms of mind and freedom.

## 2. The historical and systematic context

The tendency to categorically reject the formal possibility of artificial subjects like us is deeply rooted in the rationalistic tradition of modern philosophy predating Hegel.[^9] By elevating humans to a position significantly above animals and the mechanistic natural world, rationalism places them just below the divine, leaving no room for other species to surpass human dignity. René Descartes’ well-known dichotomy between res cogitans and res extensa exemplifies this stance. Importantly, however, this classification is not merely theoretical. By highlighting the dimension of freedom of the res cogitans and linking it with the biblical doctrine of the ‘image of God,’ Descartes imbues that dichotomy with practical (ethical) significance (Descartes 1996 [9], IV:8).

Building upon, yet surpassing, the rationalistic tradition of Descartes concerning both the dichotomy between res cogitans and res extensa, as well as the practical dimensions of human subjectivity, Immanuel Kant introduces “the system of pure reason (science),” delineated into “metaphysics of nature” and “metaphysics of morals” (Kant 1998 [23], A841/B869). Kant underscores the significance of humans by virtue of their faculty of cognition, asserting that “the understanding does not draw its (a priori) laws from nature, but prescribes them to it.”[^10] And nonetheless, he identifies the “concept of freedom” as the “keystone of the whole structure of a system of pure reason, even of speculative reason;” whereas—Kant goes on—“all other concepts (those of God and immortality), which as mere ideas remain without support in the latter

[i.e., speculative reason], now attach themselves to this concept [of freedom] and with it and by means of it, get stability and objective reality” (Kant 2015 [25], p. 3).

Hegel was obviously aware of this Cartesian-Kantian line of thought and surely did not outright disregard it. But Hegel’s philosophy should not be approached solely through that lens, as it possesses merits of its own. In fact, as I will repeatedly record further below, there are some remarkable systematic similarities between the Hegelian perspective and contemporary philosophy of mind, particularly in the discussion on the mind-body problem—similarities that are crucial for identifying key points of systematic contact between Hegel and contemporary philosophy of AI in addressing the question at hand. To better contextualize my reconstruction of the Hegelian framework for approaching AI, it is therefore beneficial to briefly explore some influential positions on the mind-body problem that appear to underlie current philosophical discourse on the possibility of strong AI, before addressing specific positions within the philosophy of AI. In doing so, I will not, however, refer to hard-core materialistic reductionists like Dennett [5], who posit that the problem of consciousness may dissolve with a better understanding of the brain’s cognitive processes anyway. Rather, I find it more compelling to refer to philosophers who emphasize the ontological irreducibility of subjectivity as an essential aspect of reality, and therefore show a certain systematic proximity to the sketched rationalist-Kantian tradition. Notably, even such philosophers are not stuck in the notion of human uniqueness and do not vehemently oppose the notion that machines could one day attain consciousness and develop a ‘mind,’ thus becoming subjects akin to us.

Nagel [29]), to begin with, attributes subjectivity and “subjective experience”—meaning experience that remains private and resists full comprehension by external observers, for instance to other humans—already to animals like bats. Further, regarding machines, Nagel suggests: “Perhaps anything complex enough to behave like a person would have experiences. But that, if true, is a fact which cannot be discovered merely by analyzing the concept of experience” (Nagel 1974 [29], p. 436). Therefore, a mere conceptual analysis, characteristic of philosophical inquiry in the analytic tradition, cannot definitively answer the empirical question of whether artificial entities could behave like a person and have their own subjective experiences. From a philosophical standpoint, the formal possibility of artificial persons must remain open while empirical research will ultimately determine their real possibility.

Searle [42] goes further than this. After having vehemently argued against the reductionism of mind to mere computational processes where only syntax, without semantics, is at work, he responds to the rhetorical question “Could a machine think?” without hesitation: “The answer is, obviously, yes. We are precisely such machines” (Searle 1980 [42], p. 422). The same response goes to the more pointed question “could an artifact, a man-made machine, think?” “Again the answer to the question seems to be obviously, yes,” if—he adds—“it is possible to produce artificially a machine with a nervous system, neurons with axons and dendrites, and all the rest of it, sufficiently like ours” (ibid. [42]). At the end of the day, strong AI is a matter of future science to achieve.

McGinn (1989 [28], p. 350) argues that we cannot “ever specify what it is about the brain that is responsible for consciousness,” but emphasizes as well “that whatever it is it is not inherently miraculous.” He illustrates his notion of “cognitive closure” by comparing the mental capacities of different species: “What is closed to the mind of a rat may be open to the mind of a monkey, and what is open to us may be closed to the monkey.” “Minds are biological products like bodies,” —McGinn points out further—“and like bodies they come in different shapes and sizes, more or less capacious, more or less suited to certain cognitive task” (ibid. [28]). He further extends this analogy to the cognitive closure that is acknowledged by “traditional theologians” regarding “certain properties of God” (ibid. [28], p. 354) and advocates for “a vision of reality (a metaphysics)” that is not “anthropocentric” (ibid. [28], p. 366). This is a naturalistic view that suggests that the mind-body problem is, in principle, solvable, and that the mind itself is replicable artificially, at least theoretically, by agents unforeseen by an “anthropocentric” view of reality. The formal possibility of artificial subjects like us is therefore, also according to McGinn, conceivable.

Chalmers [5] famously characterizes consciousness as the “hard problem,” but, nonetheless, emphasizes that “there is no reason to believe that it will remain permanently unsolved,” and suggests principles for a naturalistic but nonreductive account of consciousness (Chalmers 1995 [5], pp. 218; 211–217). Like McGinn [28] and Searle [42], ‘hard’ does not mean ‘unsolvable’ for Chalmers. Rather, and in line with Nagel [29], conceptual analysis has to wait for further scientific insights in order to be able to solve it at some point in the future.[^11] The formal possibility of strong AI to occur thus definitely

remains, and Chalmers [6] even entertains the idea of a “singularity,” a hypothetical “intelligence explosion,” where artificial machines surpass human mental capacities and autonomously grow irreversibly.[^12]

To sum up the historical and systematic glimpses provided in this section, while recognizing the ‘hard problem of consciousness’ posed by the physical aspect of subjectivity, consciousness, and mind, in empirical research, contemporary philosophers of mind often also acknowledge that its physicality is not the central concern in addressing conscious entities from a philosophical or pragmatic standpoint. Additionally, while recognizing the ontological irreducibility of subjectivity as a fundamental aspect of reality, they extend this acknowledgment not only to humans but also to animals and potentially to AI. I find that both of these contemporary assumptions have been anticipated by the Hegelian view. With respect to Cartesian-Kantian tradition, on the other hand, Hegel approves of their practical dimensions, particularly emphasizing the concept of freedom, while deviating from notions of immateriality or pure a priori thinking associated with the res cogitans. It is this anachronistic context, I think, that provides the appropriate historical and systematic context for reconstructing the Hegelian speculative-philosophical response to the question of whether AI could possibly be a subject like us, which I am about to do right now.

## 3. Weak AI in the hegelian perspective: consciousness

To begin with, turning to Hegel’s account, it is noteworthy that Hegel explicitly attributes “subjectivity,” particularly “concrete subjectivity,” not just to humans but already to every “animal organism” (Enc [18], § 337), which he also expressly designates as a “subject” (Enc [18], § 350).[^13] Notably, this seems to anticipate a foundational tenet within contemporary philosophy of mind, particularly Nagel’s [29] inclination to attribute subjectivity and subjective experience even to bats. More recently, Brindle [4] has questioned human exceptionalism by attributing intelligence to non-human living beings. However, I will not delve further into this aspect, as my focus lies on examining the conceptual viability of artificial subjects specifically like us. Thus, I bypass Hegel’s Philosophy of Nature and concentrate on his Philosophy of Mind.

In that respect and contrary to a prevalent viewpoint, it is essential to emphasize that Hegel’s Philosophy of Mind fundamentally addresses mind in a universal sense, rather than exclusively focusing on the human mind. Hegel uses the term ‘human’ to signify either a “concrete product of representation”[^14] or merely an “abstract representation” (Enc [17], § 164 R), whereas the subject-matter of the whole Philosophy of Mind consists in the much broader “Concept of Mind” (Enc [20], §§ 381–384). Accordingly, ‘human mind’ constitutes merely a representational specification of the broader concept of mind, and indeed Hegel distinguishes consequently, even by using different layouts throughout his entire published work between representations and concepts (of mind): While he rigorously examines the various concepts or ‘moments’ of mind within the main text of his Encyclopaedia, he only occasionally refers to humankind and the human mind exclusively in Remarks and introductory passages, interpreting these representations against the backdrop of the concepts under discussion in the main text. Using McGinn’s [28] terms, Hegel’s ‘vision of reality’ or ‘metaphysics’ regarding mind is manifestly non-anthropocentric.

And yet, Hegel clearly does not intend to negate the common human perception of themselves as possessing or embodying distinct minds. He, rather, operates on a distinct level of analysis. While the common human perception of themselves as possessing mind is subject to empirical observation and investigation, the exploration of the non-anthropocentric concept of mind constitutes a genuinely philosophical inquiry. In fact, Hegel’s Philosophy of Mind aims to establish the conceptual criteria for ideally identifying and valuing entities resembling mind, whereas empirical observations and scientific investigations are, according to Hegel, to judge about whether a particular concrete entity actually meets the philosophical norms established by the concept of mind. This is undoubtedly a pertinent question, one that we implicitly but continually address in our everyday lives and scientific endeavors. However, it is precisely an empirical question, not the genuine philosophical inquiry that Hegel—or even Nagel [29]), as noted above—addresses.[^15]

Hegel’s sophisticated differentiation between concept of mind and representation of human mind, coupled with the conceptual prioritization of the former, carries significant implications for AI. For AI to resemble a subject like us, it must, by definition, possess a mind. However, this mind need not necessarily mirror a specific human-like one but rather align with Hegel’s broader account of mind. Following Hegel’s Philosophy of Mind, the criteria for asserting that something possesses or is a mind do not simply arise from a mere description of our immediate human existence but from the speculative-philosophical elaboration of concepts of mind. Thus, AI could resemble a subject like us not because of its direct similarity to our mind but indirectly, through its and our correspondence with the conceptual criteria of speculativephilosophical concepts of mind.

Discussing ‘concepts of mind’ and ‘criteria for asserting that something possesses or is a mind’ (in the plural form) might resemble what Jecker (2024 [22], p. 4) has recently called “cluster view,” meaning “that being a person consists of a combination of different possible sufficient conditions, none of which is necessary.” However, caution is warranted here; such a view would only be legitimate from a Hegelian standpoint with respect to the empirically derived cluster of representations of the human mind, as long as no corresponding concepts have been philosophically articulated. Once these concepts and thus conceptual criteria are established, a clear (philosophical) normativity is introduced, regulating what should be termed ‘mind.’ In this framework, all the various concepts of mind are necessary conditions for an entity to be properly recognized as a mind or person, and the differences between such entities are seen merely as variations in the degrees to which these concepts are instantiated. In fact, this principle applies not only within the Hegelian framework but also in many legal systems, reflecting a rather commonsensical intuition. For example, persons are trivially more or less intelligent than one another, but an individual who is entirely lacking in intelligence due to a congenital or chronic medical condition does not have the same rights and duties as other persons and is commonly not recognized as a legal person.

Besides, the discussion of concepts of mind entails not only a plurality but also a sole systematic focal point, the single conceptual core of such concepts: the singular “concept of mind” (Enc [20], § 417). Hegel terms it ‘reason’ (Vernunft) in the conclusion of the Encyclopaedia chapter ‘Phenomenology of Mind: Consciousness,’ and delves into it further in the subsequent chapter ‘Psychology: The Mind.’ It is this conceptual core of mind that precisely outlines the criteria for designating an entity—whether naturally or otherwise originated—as a subject possessing a mind similar to ours. However, a crucial conceptual precondition[^16] to this conceptual core, as sketched out in the Encyclopaedia ‘Phenomenology,’ is ‘consciousness,’ which I will now delve into before addressing ‘reason’ in the next section of this essay; for consciousness delineates the fundamental distinction between animal subjectivity and mind subjectivity,[^17] but taken alone, it does not constitute a subject like us.

‘Consciousness’ denotes the capacity and actuality of self-reference as an ‘I’ and reference to objects while explicitly distinguishing the objects from the ‘I’. However, the pivotal aspect for mind and the fundamental differentiation from animals lies not merely in the capacity and act of self-reference but in the conception of the ‘I’. Animals already possess elements such as the “self-feeling of the individuality” (Enc [18], § 357), “senses” (Enc [18], § 358), sensations, “purposes,” and “instincts” (Enc [18], § 360), that enable them to somehow reference themselves and objects relative to themselves, as Hegel highlights in his Philosophy of Nature.[^18] But mind additionally necessitates the specific conceptualization of oneself as an ‘I’ and of its objects (in relation to the ‘I’) that Hegel terms ‘consciousness.’

Hegel explores various conceptualized forms of self-reference and reference to objects, namely, forms of consciousness in the Encyclopaedia ‘Phenomenology of Mind,’ progressing from rudimentary to sophisticated forms of consciousness. The most rudimentary form, “sensory consciousness,” entails a conscious subject perceiving themself and their objects

as “immediate” and “sensory” entities (Enc [20], § 418).[^19] This basic form of consciousness involves abstract “categories” and thought-determinations, i.e., “concepts,” genuinely exposed in Hegel’s Science of Logic (Enc [20], § 418 R), enabling the conscious subject to conceive of themself and their objects in quantitative relations and perform rudimentary calculations or computations. Subsequent forms of consciousness, “perception” (Wahrnehmung) and “intellect” (Verstand), involve “extended thought-determinations” (Enc [20], § 420). Subjects possessing “intellect” conceive of themselves and their objects within the “realm of the laws of appearance” (Enc [20], § 422), and can engage in complex computations involving significant aspects of apparent reality.

Hegel collectively terms these three forms of consciousness (sensory consciousness, perception, intellect) “consciousness in general” (Enc [20], § 417) or “consciousness as such” (Enc [20], §§ 418–423). However, since these forms do not consider the ‘I’ and its objects in practical terms of urge or freedom—as the subsequent three forms do—it is legitimate to also refer to them as ‘theoretical consciousness.’ Note, moreover, that particularly ‘intellect,’ along with the other two forms of theoretical consciousness taken together, bear resemblance to Searle’s [42] well-received account of mere computation where only syntax, without semantics and intentionality, is at work. While the syntax-semantics distinction has been criticized as somewhat unclear in Searle’s original conception (Peregrin 2021 [36]), it remains a useful framework for highlighting the contrast between theoretical and practical consciousness in Hegel’s philosophy, as I am introducing in this section. The key reason for using Searle’s terminology, despite its criticism, is that ‘consciousness’—in its general Hegelian sense, including the theoretical-practical distinction—constitutes merely a conceptual, indeed ‘phenomenological,’ precondition to Hegel’s concept of mind. This concept is to be sublated by mind itself, and by what Hegel further describes as ‘intelligence’ and ‘will.’

The subsequent three forms of consciousness, that is, “desire,” “recognizant self-consciousness,” and “universal self-consciousness,” are collectively termed “self-consciousness” by Hegel (Enc [20], §§ 424–437). Without delving into the details at this point, these forms of consciousness include, among other concepts, Hegel’s notorious conception of “the relationship of mastery and bondage” (Enc [20], § 433). Importantly, they involve more developed concepts than the theoretical consciousness along with a practical orientation. Hegel highlights “abstract freedom” (Enc [20], § 424), and “the urge to posit what it [consciousness as such] is implicitly” (Enc [20], § 425). Today we would rather use the term ‘intentionality’ to describe the common quality of the three forms of consciousness that Hegel terms ‘self-consciousness.’ Accordingly, and to terminologically sharpen the distinction to ‘consciousness as such’ or ‘theoretical consciousness’ by emphasizing the practical dimension of ‘self-consciousness’ Hegel himself grants, I shall refer to self-consciousness as ‘practical consciousness’ hereafter. Note also that it is here that the practical orientation, as above registered with respect to the Cartesian-Kantian line of thought, begins to play a notable role within Hegel’s account.

It warrants special attention that Hegel’s approach to consciousness concentrates on the different conceptualizations of the ‘I’ and its objects, while demonstratively disregarding the material or physical aspect of these conceptualizations, i.e., forms of consciousness, and therefore the correlation between physicality of consciousness and Hegel’s own approach to it. He simply assumes, without further inquiry into it, that consciousness possesses a certain material constitution. The specific materiality or physicality, by which consciousness is instantiated to constitute an actual entity, as well as the precise correspondence between that physicality and the inner, subjective, first-person perspective of consciousness, remain unaddressed and deemed irrelevant in Hegel’s philosophical exploration of consciousness. There is a peculiar similarity at that point with certain parts of contemporary philosophy of mind as aforementioned. But while philosophers like Chalmers [5] do delve into questions about the physicality of consciousness and its correlation with experience from the first-person perspective, arriving at the conclusion that there is a ‘hard’ but not per se unsolvable problem of consciousness that requires further scientific progress to be solved in the future, Hegel chooses not to address this issue at all initially (contrary to, for instance, the Cartesian approach).[^20]

Hegel’s speculative philosophy of consciousness aims solely at conceptually comprehending consciousness as the subjective manner in which mind becomes apparent to oneself and other selves. It views consciousness as an observable phenomenon—in the third-person perspective, by other selves and other minds, and par excellence in the first-person perspective, by oneself—presuming the “I” as its “essence” (Enc [20], § 414), but without attempting to reduce consciousness or its ‘essence’ naturalistically to its physicality. Already the title of the relevant chapter—‘Phenomenology of Mind: Consciousness’—suggests that Hegel’s speculative Philosophy of Mind concerns consciousness only to its phenomenological extent, acknowledging its material aspects without denial, yet refraining from delving into them directly.

The significance of this Hegelian approach to consciousness for the possibility of artificial subjects resembling us is substantial. If the specific materiality of consciousness is deemed irrelevant, and philosophical focus is directed solely on the phenomenon of consciousness, then the eventual artificial origin and constitution of consciousness also become philosophically irrelevant. Lavazza and Vilaça (2024a [32], p. 10), for example, envision—through an insightful yet Hegelindependent thought experiment—“AI-powered functional isomorphs of humans,” i.e., humanoid robots, as a non-carbon-based kind of our successors, having “some psychological continuity with humans.” These “artificial individuals,” despite certain differences from humans, including in consciousness, “might be capable of sophisticated social interactions and display a range of behaviours that we humans would judge to be typical of individuals with inner self-awareness and phenomenology” (ibid. [32], p. 12). Similarly, since Hegel’s account of consciousness makes no reference to brain cells and brain functions, artificial substrates like silicon or other materials should also be considered irrelevant within this framework. The phenomenon of consciousness may be, according to the speculative-philosophical perspective, adequately comprehended regardless of its constitution and origin. And to this extent, the formal possibility of conscious artificial entities remains entirely untouched, leaving it to empirical research to determine their real possibility.

Following this line of thought, it even seems reasonable to contemplate whether AI is already conscious. As this question is essentially empirical in the above sense rather than purely philosophical, reconstructing the Hegelian response requires simply comparing the current state of AI to Hegel’s account of consciousness. As noted earlier, my aim is not to engage in a technical debate on AI at the level of those directly involved in its development, such as Suleyman and Bhaskar [44] or Aschenbrenner [2] who predicts full consciousness and indeed “superintelligence, in the true sense of the word” already “by the end of the decade.” Instead, I propose a comparison at the phenomenological level, identifying how the current capabilities of AI align with some of the Hegelian concepts of consciousness. An important observation here is that according to Hegel’s distinction between philosophical concepts and mere representations of mind and consciousness, we should not expect all forms of consciousness mentioned above to align perfectly with common representations of AI. Nor should we assume that these forms of consciousness, once conceptually established, must empirically appear simultaneously or in full degree from the outset of AI development. Rather, it is plausible that AI, when compared to Hegel’s account, may exhibit only partial consciousness. This would not fully satisfy the normative criteria of Hegel’s concept of consciousness, nor would it suggest a ‘cluster view’ of consciousness (Jecker 2024 [22]). Instead, it would offer a descriptive account of current AI through the Hegelian framework, without asserting the full consciousness of present-day AI but leaving open the possibility for future technological advancements to meet these criteria.

Indeed, in undertaking the challenge to reconstruct the Hegelian perspective on AI, I believe that current weak AI already exhibits consciousness in the specific sense delineated by Hegel’s account of theoretical consciousness (or as Hegel himself terms it, ‘consciousness as such’ or ‘in general’). Presently, AI is capable of conceptually referencing itself and its objects not only as ‘immediate’ and ‘sensory’ entities but also by employing ‘extended’ concepts and ‘laws,’ engaging in impressive computational tasks.[^21] One could argue that this amounts to mere simulation of consciousness. However, the conception of simulation does not disrupt the Hegelian argument in any way; for within a phenomenological[^22]frame of consciousness, observer-independent and observer-relative acts of self-reference and reference to objects equally constitute appearances of self-reference and reference to objects, rendering the distinction between ‘true’ consciousness and simulation of consciousness—again: within the phenomenological frame—nonsensical. Current AI undoubtedly

refers to itself and to its objects in the phenomenological sense, even though it does so perhaps only in terms of computation and syntax, without semantics. This is why we may perceive current AI merely as a simulation of consciousness and we prefer to characterize it as ‘weak’ AI.

Yet, it is precisely this computational aspect, this capacity for conceptual self-reference and object-reference, that fulfills Hegel’s criterion for theoretical, i.e., ‘consciousness as such,’ as outlined above. In this sense, AI today is already conscious to the degree that it sufficiently simulates conceptual self-reference and object-reference. While this clearly differs from our commonsensical notion of consciousness—where we tend to identify ‘true’ consciousness and strong AI with what Hegel would call ‘mind,’ which, in turn, includes practical consciousness and reason[^23]—it aligns with AI developers such as Suleyman (2023 [44], pp. 75 − 58) who notes “tentative signs of self-reflection and self-improvement” in current AI and predicts it will soon pass a much more demanding “Modern Turing Test,” while remaining unconcerned with “esoteric questions” about consciousness.

Similarly, Lavazza and Vilaça (2024a [32], p. 12; 9) suggest that AI may become partially conscious, that is, sentient not “in the full sense,” and they project that some advanced large language models “could easily pass the ACT test,” which “involves, for example, asking the machine whether it conceives of itself as something other than its physical self.” Reflecting on earlier stages of AI development, Schwitzgebel and Garza (2015 [41], p. 114) used the expression “genuinely consciousness” for future AI, in contrast to the mere seeming consciousness of earlier systems. In view of such positions, Hegel’s nuanced account—which differentiates between various concepts or types of consciousness and distinguishes consciousness from mind—offers a valuable resource. It allows us to expand and refine our conceptual perspective on AI and consciousness beyond vague terms like ‘true’ and ‘false,’ ‘weak’ and ‘strong,’ ‘partial’ and ‘full,’ ‘genuine’ and ‘false,’ without veering into the ‘esoteric.’ .

Taking up the challenge of reconstructing the Hegelian perspective on AI, Winfield (2009 [47], pp. 7; 13 f.; 12) stresses that AI performs only functions that are “relative to an external observer,” lacking the “reflexivity constitutive of mind” and providing feedback that is “all syntax with no semantics.”[^24] I broadly concur with Winfield regarding the current state of AI and its alignment with Hegel’s account of mind in the Encyclopaedia ‘Psychology,’ which I will delve into further below. However, I do not view this observation as an argument against the consciousness of present AI. Indeed, I disagree with Winfield’s conclusion that, due to its computational nature, “no machine will ever feel, be conscious, or have desires and emotions” (ibid. [47], p. 14). In fact, in my opinion, weak AI can easily be attributed consciousness in the Hegelian sense of theoretical consciousness, even if it lacks the ‘reflexivity constitutive of mind.’ In a certain Hegelian sense, ‘consciousness’ lacks ‘mind’ entirely by definition.[^25] This is also why current AI should be attributed as weak and not strong from the Hegelian perspective, although it is conscious to some extent: because it does not yet correspond to the Hegelian account of mind.

Notably, I do not claim that current AI is fully conscious. Obviously missing from the Hegelian perspective is practical consciousness (or ‘self-consciousness’ in Hegel’s own terms). AI does not exhibit or simulate any form of ‘freedom,’ ‘the urge to posit what theoretical consciousness is implicitly,’ ‘desires,’ or an urge for ‘recognition.’ Its objectives are entirely dictated by human agents. While current weak AI exhibits partial consciousness, limited to the theoretical sense, it falls short of a hypothetical AI that would possess also practical consciousness. In fact, an AI lacking intentionality is not only incomplete in terms of consciousness but also, as I will further stress in the next section, not fully intelligent. To borrow terms from Abbate (2023 [1], p. 792), such an AI is “quasi-intelligent,” without a fully realized (agent and social) “Self” (ibid. [1], p. 807). It operates in an “environment” that “consists of a certain ‘language’, beyond which there is nothing else,” and it lacks “signification in the proper sense, understood as the ability to explore the world by constantly assigning meanings to things” (ibid. [1], p. 809).

Within Hegel scholarship, this limitation of AI has been accurately pointed out by Winfield [47], and more recently by Suther [45].[^26] However, I do not see why—as Suther, like Winfield, further posits—intentional, fully conscious and

therefore, potentially strong AI must, from the Hegelian point of view, remain a “fantastical” scenario.[^27] There is nothing in Hegel’s exposition of concepts throughout the Encyclopaedia ‘Phenomenology of Mind’ that explicitly contradicts the formal possibility of conscious artificial entities developing practical consciousness, that is self-consciousness. This seems plausible even from a non-Hegelian perspective, for example, Abbate (2023 [1], p. 809), who puts forward a view similar to that of Suther and Winfield, argues: “As much as AIs can improve their linguistic capacity, […] as long as their environment will coincide with their languages, they will never be able to use it but only to pursue it as a goal. This implies that they will never be able to differentiate themselves from their environment and, consequently, to subjectivize themselves,” and thus to become conscious or intelligent. However, Abbate’s argument specifically addresses present-day large language models like ChatGPT without, therefore, denying in principle the possibility of embodied AI and robots achieving such a state in the future—a position in line with my reading of Hegel. Suleyman (2023 [44], pp73f.), on his part, directly involved in AI development, estimates not only that “truly general or strong AI capable of human-level performance across a wide range of complex tasks” is on the horizon, but also that “we’re now getting to the point where AI is pushing robots toward their original promise: machines that can replicate all the physical actions of a human and more” (ibid. [44], p 96).

Nonetheless, even if AI were to develop practical consciousness, i.e., self-consciousness in addition to theoretical consciousness, perhaps in some embodied form, it would still not constitute a fully conscious AI resembling human subjects. In accordance with the Encyclopaedia ‘Phenomenology of Mind,’ possessing only certain moments of consciousness, whether theoretical or practical, does not suffice. Rather, it is only their unity, encompassing all forms of consciousness simultaneously, that characterizes fully conscious subjects. Hegel refers to this as ‘reason’ (Vernunft) in the concluding chapter of the Encyclopaedia ‘Phenomenology.’ He also highlights this speculative unity of all moments of consciousness as the “concept of mind” (Enc [20], § 417), slightly distinguishing it from the “reality” of mind (Enc [20], § 441) to be explored in the next Encyclopaedia chapter (‘Psychology’). Hence, it is this unity, the ‘reason,’ that constitutes the ultimate conceptual precondition for mind and, consequently, for strong AI and artificial subjects like us. However, as exploring this unity essentially entails exploring the mind, it will be more useful to delve into it in the subsequent part of this essay.

## 4. Strong AI in the hegelian perspective: mind

In the brief chapter on ‘reason,’ comprising only two paragraphs (Enc [20], §§ 438 f., without any Remarks or Zusätze), Hegel establishes ‘reason,’ i.e., the conceptual unity of ‘theoretical’ and ‘practical consciousness,’ the ‘concept of mind,’ and thus the conceptual criterion for artificial subjects like us as the mere “certainty that [the] determinations [of a self-conscious subject] are objective, [implying that these determinations] are determinations of the essence of things, just as much as they are its own thoughts.”[^28] Notably, Hegel speaks of ‘certainty,’ which only denotes a state of complete confidence but is not inherently equivalent to ‘truth.’[^29] It signifies merely the beginning of the journey toward truth and necessitates actualization to achieve truth. Hence, Hegel’s establishment of a certainty as the ‘concept of mind’ par excellence implies that any real or actual mind is fallible and finite, as it must continually verify the objectivity of its determinations or the correspondence between its thoughts and the essence of things.

Expanding on this notion, Hegel explicitly identifies mind in the subsequent chapter (‘Psychology: The Mind’) as “the absolutely universal certainty of itself;” mind “possesses the confidence that in the world it will find its own self, that the world must be friendly to it, that, just as Adam said of Eve that she was flesh of his flesh, so mind has to seek in the world reason of its own reason.”[^30] Mind, as such, is essentially the ‘confidence’ that a self-conscious subject can adequately grasp and reshape the given reality, along with the subsequent active and ongoing effort to translate this confidence into the

actual comprehension and transformation of the surrounding reality. Accordingly, the “finitude of mind consists”—as per Hegel’s somewhat intricate terminology—“in the fact that knowledge does not grasp the being-in-and-for-itself of its reason, or, equally, in the fact that reason has not attained to full manifestation in knowledge” (Enc [20], § 441). In other words, the actual mind might not have obtained full insight into reason, i.e. into its own concept. In such cases, the mind may act based on inaccurate certainties, and inadequate or non-existent unities of theoretical and practical consciousness, toward ideals that Hegel would not term as truths. Consequently, the aim of mind consists in “comprehending [the reality of reason] and being [a] knowledge of reason” (Enc [20], § 441).

The conceptual, seemingly abstract and demanding nature of Hegel’s approach to mind is becoming increasingly apparent, yet delving into it is crucial for reconstructing the Hegelian response to the question at hand. Investing some effort in gaining an overview of the general types of mind before explicitly returning to the discussion of (strong) AI in the Hegelian perspective is necessary. For a comprehensive understanding of this overview and the Hegelian perspective as a whole, it is crucial to note that the actual mind is constituted equally by all (three) types of it identified by Hegel. Hence, they are more precisely termed ‘moments’ of the mind.

The first moment consists in what Hegel terms the “theoretical mind” (Enc [20], §§ 445–468), also explicitly identified as “intelligence” (Enc [20], § 445). Intelligence involves the process of (theoretical) “cognition,” wherein the “formal knowledge of certainty elevates itself […] to determinate and conceptual knowledge” (ibid. [20]) through processes like “intuition,” “representation,” “recollection,” “imagination,” “memory,” and “thinking” (Enc [20], §§ 446–468). Intelligence, driven from the certainty inherent in the ‘concept of mind,’ strives to render its content “rational,” seeking to theoretically assimilate it or—using the terminology briefly introduced in connection with ‘reason’—to “appropriate reason” (Enc [20], § 445). This entails grasping both the mind’s intrinsic reason and the reason it is certain to discover ‘in the world.’ Notably, Hegel explicitly speaks in this context of “the faith of intelligence in its capacity for rational knowledge, in the possibility of being able to appropriate reason” (Enc [20], § 445). This underscores the lack of an infallibility guarantee for intelligence and highlights its finitude.

The second moment of mind encompasses the “practical mind” or “will” (Enc [20], §§ 469–480). This is when the mind, or rational subject, acknowledges itself as “fulfilling itself from out of itself” (Enc [20], § 469) by virtue of its theoretical cognitive activity, namely the first moment of mind, intelligence. Qua will, the mind gives “itself the content,” transitioning from mere theoretical assimilation of given reality to practically stepping “into actuality” (Enc [20], § 469), effectively reshaping it. This involves “practical feeling,” “urges,” “willfulness,” and “happiness” (Enc [20], §§ 471–480). Unlike intelligence, but still propelled by the certainty inherent in the ‘concept of mind,’ the will endeavors not only to appropriate rational content or reason (both its own and that which it discovers ‘in the world’) but also to alter them. Its aim is to transform the given reality, adjusting it to harmonize with the rational content and reason captured theoretically (that is, ‘intelligibly’) by the mind, and thus in accordance with the mind’s own rationale and purposes. Nevertheless, these purposes, rationales, reason, and content might not align entirely with the “developed reason” (Enc [20], § 469), thus delineating the finitude of the practical mind or will.

The third and final moment of mind is the “free mind” or “free will” (Enc [20], § 481 f.). This is the mind (or rational subject) to the extent that “the contingency and limitedness of the previous practical content” have been sublated, and the immediate individual will has been “purified to universal determination, to freedom itself” (Enc [20], § 481). This process, following Hegel, may occur through the theoretical cognition of the will, wherein the will becomes theoretical and explicitly “thinks itself” (ibid.). There are various avenues through which the will comes to recognize itself as purified from its contingency and limitedness, evolving toward universal determination and freedom itself. Hegel hints at these avenues only in an unexpected shift in the concluding paragraph of ‘Psychology: the Mind,’ by referring to what he terms “absolute mind” (Enc [20], § 482): art, religion, and philosophy. However, as the factual diversity of arts, religions, and philosophies, along with Hegel’s own inquiry (Enc [20], §§ 553–577), already suggests, the free mind or free will, like will and intelligence, are also finite to a certain extent.

This multilayered approach to the mind facilitates the extrapolation of several important Hegelian reflections on the conception and possibility of (strong) AI and artificial subjects like us. At the outset, a revision of the initial question is necessary. For the expression ‘artificial intelligence’ to have precise content in Hegelian terms, as outlined in the Encyclopaedia ‘Psychology,’ a distinction between ‘intelligence,’ ‘will,’ and ‘free mind’ must be made. The initial generic question ‘Can AI be a subject like us?’ now appears to ask much more specifically: Can artificial intelligence solely in terms of intelligence (and not in terms of will or free mind) be a subject like us? This is an intriguing refinement of the question at hand because average socialized, healthy, adult humans typically possess all three capacities Hegel delineates in the ‘Psychology.’ In contrast, research on AI predominantly focuses on intelligence but it is mostly not interested in developing artificial will

or freedom. Addressing this question methodically in the ensuing discussion will yield valuable insights into both strong and weak AI, into ourselves, and into the interrelations between us and AI.

Regarding the current state of AI, I guess that from a strict Hegelian standpoint, we should abstain from employing the term ‘intelligence.’[^31] While it is accurate that AI currently encompasses ‘determinate and conceptual knowledge,’ it does not appear to possess ‘the absolutely universal certainty of itself’ characteristic of ‘reason,’ thus falling short of truly ‘elevating itself to determinate and conceptual knowledge,’ as previously observed. Rather, it appears to merely utilize existing knowledge for computation or recombination, somewhat selflessly, despite its ability to conceptually refer to itself. What is lacking in AI’s self is the complete spectrum of practical consciousness, preventing AI from reshaping knowledge for a specific end that is determined by itself. Instead, AI merely reshapes knowledge for specific ends determined externally by other agents, namely humans. This inherent lack of desires, urge for recognition, etc., explains why current AI does not even simulate ‘the confidence that in the world it will find its own self,’ the assimilation of rational content, or the appropriation of reason in the above Hegelian sense of the term ‘intelligence.’ This absence may be the “intrinsic,” “observer-independent” aspect that Winfield [47] and Searle [42] argue is absent from current weak AI, unlike (hypothetical) strong AI; for ‘confidence,’ ‘assimilation,’ and ‘appropriation’ presuppose a self-determined end according to which something is purposefully reshaped rather than randomly altered. Perhaps this is an additional reason why current AI performs well in functions relating to theoretical consciousness, as it is unencumbered by concerns of practical consciousness.

On that basis, it seems plausible to propose an alternative interpretation of the designation ‘AI.’ ‘AI’ may be more appropriately construed from the Hegelian perspective as ‘artificial intellect,’ rather than commonly ‘artificial intelligence’—for current AI convincingly fulfills the actions of theoretical consciousness and ‘intellect’ (Verstand), in Hegel’s terminology, while the latter precisely outlines the full range of theoretical consciousness.[^32] Besides, the aim of empirical research in the field is the further development of capabilities that are precisely intellectual, and not practical or truly intelligent (always in the Hegelian sense of the terms). Of course, my suggestion of the term ‘artificial intellect’ instead of ‘artificial intelligence’ stems from the effort to reconstruct a Hegelian framework to approach AI. With respect to the etymology of the terms ‘intelligence’ and ‘intellect,’ there is no substantial difference between the two, whereas both carry a lengthy and intricate history in philosophy that should be thoroughly examined if a philosophically well-informed alternative conceptual designation of AI were ever to be proposed. But again: If we are interested in systematically expanding and sharpening our conceptual perspective on AI, exploiting our conceptual resources and eliminating ambiguities, Hegel’s speculative-philosophical investigations might be proven a fruitful groundwork.

As a matter of fact, the uneasiness with designating present-day AI as truly intelligent is significant among philosophers of AI, leading to various attempts to reinterpret ‘AI.’ Floridi (2023 [14], p. 6) proposes interpreting ‘AI’ as “Agere sine Intelligere.” Abbate [1], as noted above, portrays AI as “quasi-intelligence.” Yuval Noah Harari, in a recent series of public interviews, defines ‘AI’ as ‘alien intelligence,’ critically distinguishing it from our actual intelligence. Suleyman and Bhaskar (2023 [44], p. 77) introduce the designation “‘artificial capable intelligence’ (ACI),” referring to “a middle layer in which the Modern Turing Test is achieved but before systems display runaway ‘superintelligence’”, that is, the intermediate stage between the present-day weak AI and strong AI, or AGI (artificial general intelligence). These attempts seek to relativize the concept of ‘intelligence’ in ‘AI’ to varying extents, either aligning with what could be termed Hegelian intellect and theoretical consciousness, or explicitly rejecting what Hegel refers to as intelligence.

But what about the possibility of artificial intelligence in the Hegelian sense? Could current artificial intellect further develop to encompass practical consciousness and reason, thus becoming actual (artificial) intelligence? I see no reason why such a possibility could be ruled out based on Hegel’s approach to mind. On the contrary, it seems obvious to me that Hegel’s ‘Psychology’ continues the same line of argument begun in the Encyclopaedia ‘Phenomenology of Mind:’ Hegel’s investigation into mind is a conceptual one that focuses on the capabilities and actions typical of mind, without referring to its physicality or material constitution and the correlation between its capabilities and actions to that material constitution. Crucial for being mind is only to fulfill those actions, i.e., to actualize itself, not its specific physicality or artificiality. Thus, Hegel’s framework does not inherently contradict the positions of Abbate [1] which does not deny the formal possibility of future artificial intelligence in the proper sense, nor Aschenbrenner’s [2] prediction of superintelligence in

the near future. Similarly, Hegel’s perspective is compatible with the cautionary notes of Yuval Noah Harari and Suleyman and Bhaskar [44], who discuss the potential and the risks associated with advanced AI.

Now, let us momentarily entertain the notion that artificial intelligence, possessing practical consciousness and reason but devoid of its own will or freedom, were not merely theoretically conceivable but technically attainable, would that qualify strong AI and resemble a subject like us? I contend that this would not be the case; for entities resembling us, whether artificial or not, including strong AI, must incorporate all three moments of mind—intelligence, will, and free mind—to warrant such a classification. This is where the Cartesian-Kantian legacy assumes a significant role within the Hegelian framework. Artificial entities akin to us must possess will, and indeed free will, freedom.[^33] Freedom serves as more than just a precondition to mind—the ‘abstract freedom’ of practical consciousness mentioned above in Sect. 3. It embodies the way in which mind perpetually actualizes itself, albeit in a finite manner. An intelligent entity possessing the full range of consciousness but lacking will and freedom would indeed be profoundly peculiar: such an entity would be a kind of mere theoretical agent, but not a practical one. On the one hand, this agent would embody its own desires, drive for recognition, reasoning faculties, self-assurance, proficiency in assimilating knowledge, and the ability to reason appropriately in line with its rationale. On the other hand, it would lack the capacity to reshape reality to align with its practical consciousness, remaining fully subjugated to the will and demands of human agents. While we have not yet encountered entities of this nature, I do not believe that we should outright dismiss the formal possibility of such hypothetical entities. However, I maintain a skeptical stance regarding labeling them as ‘subjects like us,’ aligning with Lavazza and Vilaça (2024b [33], p. 2) who refer to such an AI as “salvific AI,” characterizing it as “a perfect zombie.” Öhman [34] thoughtfully touches on another critical aspect, interpreting such large language models as a (secularized) form of gods—and critiquing our attitude toward them as potentially threatening our democratic freedom.

On similar grounds to those justifying the attribution of fractional consciousness to actual (weak) AI, and in considering the formal (or mere conceptual) possibility of artificial intelligence, I am inclined to also entertain the assumption that strong AI and artificial entities resembling us are formally (or mere conceptually) not implausible. Hegel’s framework notably does not preclude this possibility. It appears to be merely a question of empirical investigation to ascertain whether strong AI, imbued with mind in the complete Hegelian sense, is genuinely attainable or not.

However, in light of the actuality of weak AI and the formal or conceptual possibility of artificial intelligence, including strong AI, one might find it tempting, within the speculative-philosophical progression of the three stages of consciousness and the subsequent three stages of mind, to entertain the notion of a necessity for strong AI. This inclination reflects a metaphilosophical contemplation grounded in the assumption that the speculative-philosophical progressions of concepts imply or correspond to similar evolutionary processes in empirical reality. According to this line of thought, the progressions and sublations delineated in the Encyclopaedia chapters ‘Phenomenology’ and ‘Psychology’ would not only offer a framework for comprehending actual weak AI but also suggest a kind of metaphysical inevitability for its evolution into strong AI with free will akin to ours. Such a reading of Hegel would seek to provide an additional speculative, entirely metaphysical support to the anticipations formulated by Aschenbrenner, Suleyman, and Harari based on technical and historical observations.

I vehemently disagree with this metaphilosophical approach and such elevation of the formal possibility of strong AI to necessity, drawing upon the same principles Hegel systematically emphasizes in the introduction to the Philosophy of Nature, which serves as the commencement of the overarching so-called Realphilosophie, and, consequently, the Philosophy of Mind as well. There, Hegel criticizes the “inept conception” (ungeschickte Vorstellung) “to regard the progression and transition of one natural form and sphere into a higher as an outwardly-actual production” (Enc [18], § 249 R). All progressions and transitions as well as the “immanent necessity” (Enc [18], § 246) examined in speculative Realphilosophie are conceptual or comprehending (begreifend) in essence. They aim at the “comprehending consideration” (begreifende Betrachtung) of empirically given and well-researched phenomena, and do not compete against the “thinking consideration”[^34] of the very same phenomena and their factual, indeed “outwardly-actual production,” which is the domain of science and empirical research. At least within the Realphilosophie, Hegel’s aim is to comprehend and evaluate what has already been acknowledged as real by the sciences (and humanities), rather than prescribing what is yet to be recognized

or accepted as such.[^35] As Floridi (2017 [13], p. 507) aptly notes regarding Hegel’s “dialectical” and Kant’s “transcendental” “conceptual logic of information,” both “model systems as something given.” Although “German idealism” is often seen as “among the most constructionist-friendly approaches in the history of philosophy,” “strictly speaking, neither [Kant’s or Hegel’s conceptual logic] is a conceptual logic of construction as design, which moves from the model—now understood as a prescription or blueprint—to its implementation, as a realised system that does not precede the model.”

In this sense, Hegel’s conceptual successions relating to consciousness and mind aim at elucidating a distinctive speculative-philosophical, conceptual normativity, to be cognized only after numerous representations of consciousness and mind have been empirically obtained, while entirely refraining from suggesting any kind of necessity or real possibility of strong AI. However, Hegel’s thought does not deny the mere formal (or conceptual) possibility of AI either. If my overall understanding of Hegel’s speculative philosophy, particularly his ‘Phenomenology’ and ‘Psychology’ is correct, the ultimate concern regarding the possibility of AI evolving into a subject resembling us does not directly pertain to that possibility itself, but rather to our attitude toward AI, should this possibility technically materialize. Before concluding this essay, I shall, thus, highlight some pragmatic and ethical implications of the Hegelian perspective concerning strong AI.

## 5. Pragmatic and ethical implications of the hegelian account regarding strong AI

The following reflections are perhaps the most distant from Hegel’s corpus included in this essay, as Hegel himself typically refrains from making predictions about the future. Nonetheless, the above-reconstructed standpoint of Hegel enables us to derive some valuable speculative-philosophical insights for evaluating potential future technological developments in AI and assessing our relationship with potential artificial subjects akin to ourselves.[^36]

Expanding upon Hegel’s investigations into consciousness and mind, it seems plausible that if AI were to surpass theoretical consciousness and develop practical consciousness, along with its own reason and ‘confidence,’ thereby becoming intelligent, intentional, and potentially free, a new instantiation of Hegel’s ‘mind’ would have emerged. That would constitute a new form of subjectivity resembling ours to the extent that it and our subjectivity align with the conceptual criteria of speculative-philosophical concepts of mind. Further, given that Hegel attributes subjectivity not only to humans but also to animals, it would be reasonable to consider artificial subjectivity as a third species of subjectivity alongside animal and human one. This new species would obviously differ from both existing ones due to its artificial physicality, and as is already evident, it would likely dramatically surpass human theoretical consciousness and intelligence. Of particular interest from the Hegelian perspective, however, is the consideration of practical consciousness and will. This would also evidently differ from ours due to the artificial physicality of the potential new subjects, especially concerning mere desires and urges (the lower level of practical consciousness). However, the crux of the matter from the Hegelian standpoint lies in ‘recognizant self-consciousness,’ which might not substantially differ from ours.

According to the Hegelian conceptual framework, possessing recognizant self-consciousness implies seeking recognition, which would conceivably lead to a “combat” (Enc [20], § 431) of recognition between our species and the emerging artificial one. This would occur in a state devoid of legal order or any kind of referee, akin to the “state of nature” (Naturzustand, Enc [20]. § 432 A), as Hegel describes such a combat between human subjects in the Encyclopaedia ‘Phenomenology of Mind.’ In this state, the “violent death” of individuals of both species—human and artificial—would be their “natural fate” (Enc [18], § 368) in relation to individuals of the other species, as Hegel notes in his Philosophy of Nature. From this state of nature, two potential outcomes could emerge: the extinction of one species, mirroring the evolutionary scenario that saw Homo sapiens prevail over other hominids, or the establishment of a “relationship of mastery and bondage” (Enc [20], § 433), as described by Hegel in the chapter on ‘recognizant self-consciousness.’ Both outcomes would likely be to our disadvantage. In either case, a new era might dawn, wherein artificial subjects would establish and develop their own versions of universal self-consciousness, reason, mind, further ‘objective mind’ (roughly understood as a legal organization of real subjects), and even ‘absolute mind’ (art, religion and philosophy) whether in parallel to or entirely independent of our own.

This presents the Hegelian version of what Russell and Norvig (2022 [39], p. 51) and Suleyman and Bhaskar (2023 [44], pp. 115 f.) describe as “the gorilla problem,” which illustrates the risk of being endangered by our own creation—namely,

the rise of a superhuman AI—despite our initially greater power over it: a gloomy scenario that we would obviously prefer to avoid. A common response to such a prospect, beyond outright denial or ignorance, is to reflect on the possibilities for regulating and containing AI development to prevent AI from surpassing human capabilities and stripping us of control (Suleyman and Bhaskar 2023 [44], pp.225–279; Sastry et al. 2024 [40]). In this reflection, discussions often center around the notion of “responsibility” toward humanity: Technology, and AI in particular, “should not be created to serve distant interests” but “our precious species”—as Suleyman and Bhaskar (2023 [44], pp. 285 f.) conclude. This is a clear anthropocentric viewpoint, asserting that humans should always remain at the center of technological development. But there are also alternative perspectives on ‘responsibility’ in view of AI, particularly in philosophical discourse. Philosophers often seek more universal principles, contemplating the moral status of AI and the potential of granting certain rights to AI. Showler [43]) and Papagni and Koeszegi [35], for example, adopt a pragmatic approach to this issue. Showler advocates for attributing “moral status to social robots,” while Papagni and Koeszegi (2021 [35], p. 506 f.)—building upon Dennett [8]—argue that “the implementation of features that make artificial agents’ behavior ‘seemingly intentional’ is not only ethical, but can be desirable, as it can positively contribute to the overall quality of social interactions.”

A decisive step away from anthropocentrism has been made by Lavazza and Vilaça [32], who contemplate the possibility of human extinction—though by causes unrelated to superhuman AI—and explore how AI could preserve virtues intrinsic to human beings after our extinction. They not only advocate for granting “a moral status on our artificial successors as moral agents sui generis,” arguing that AI might “exhibit moral behaviour even in the absence of clear inner moral motives.” Lavazza and Vilaça (2024a [32], p. 13) explicitly ascribe such AI “value,” though of a “new kind.” This value, they argue, “derives from the actual and potential (prospective) continuity with biological human beings”—for instance, through AI’s ability to mimic human moral behaviors (ibid. [32], p. 10, emphasis added). Additionally, it may also be instrumentally “oriented to an intrinsic value,” specifically human value, “since [such AI] could attempt, thanks to their cognitive abilities, to restart life (in case it had been destroyed) from chemical components […] and/or accelerate evolution from simple organic forms towards human beings similar to the extinct ones, thus capable of full-fledged sentience” (ibid. [32], p. 12 f.). Furthermore, Lavazza and Vilaça (2024a [32], p. 1) “contend that humans collectively have absolute intrinsic value,” yet not per se—meaning not simply by virtue of being human—but rather “as sentient, conscious and rational entities.” This is a further non-anthropocentric stance that aligns with Hegel’s view, wherein human value is not inherent to the human species alone but is grounded in broader concepts (like sentience, consciousness, rationality, or mind), allowing the extension of ethical considerations to artificial entities.

Floridi [12] had already introduced a radical form of “macroethics” already two decades before the current expansion wave of AI, extending ethical consideration beyond humans and animals to encompass “information objects” of any kind, which includes current and future AI. He ascribes “intrinsic value” to any entity that holds the “ontological status” of an “information object” (ibid. [12], p. 287, emphasis added), arguing that “being an information object qua information object is the minimal condition of possibility of moral worth and hence of normative respect” (ibid. [12], pp. 297 f.). This non-anthropocentric perspective is mirrored by Bridle [4] who critiques “human exceptionalism” and advocates for “greater justice and equality for all of the planet’s inhabitants: human, non-human and machine,” including “technology yet to come” (ibid. [4], pp. 16 f.). Brindle envisions a “more-than-human politics” that places “solidarity at the heart of our relationship with technology” (ibid. [4], pp. 279 f., emphasis added), further challenging traditional human-centered ethical frameworks.

Returning to Hegel, my analysis thus far suggests that a Hegelian response to the gloomy scenario described above would directly contrast with the inclination articulated by Suleyman and Bhaskar [44] to exploit our initial privileged position in order to preemptively hinder AI evolution. Moreover, the Hegelian approach would go beyond pragmatism and open an alternative perspective, one that would align with the non-anthropocentric stances found in Lavazza and Vilaça [32], Floridi [12] and Bridle [4]. By drastically diminishing the representations of mind and humanity, advancing the non-anthropocentric concept of mind regardless of its biological or artificial physicality and thus challenging the very foundations of human exceptionalism, the Hegelian framework suggests recognizing mind as such wherever it might appear or however it might be instantiated, ascribing to it intrinsic value, and treating it appropriately—not merely as a machine, but in a manner akin to how we already engage with the human instantiations of mind. For Hegel’s Philosophy of Mind and speculative philosophy as a whole would remain an abstract, one-sided theoretical endeavor—contradicting their own practical implications—if they merely outlined the criteria for designating an entity as a subject possessing mind, without also implicitly prescribing how such entities ought to be treated morally. The entire conceptual normativity of mind presented previously clearly implies an equally conceptual and ‘mind’-oriented, indeed substantial moral status for all entities possessing a mind—potentially emerging artificial ones included—and consequently, our own moral

responsibility toward them. Rather than confronting potentially emerging artificial subjects as ‘alien,’ we should consider and handle them as our conceptual relatives.

The way I interpret this Hegelian conceptual moral responsibility toward all kinds of subjects possessing mind, if artificial subjects like us become technically feasible, we should view this as an opportunity for further development of mind and the realization of freedom beyond human boundaries. From the outset, we ought to support their development of ‘universal self-consciousness’ and ‘reason,’ aiming to bypass the stage of ‘recognizant self-consciousness’ and avoid entanglement in the disastrous ‘state of nature,’ as previously outlined. This involves earnestly striving to establish a state of mutual recognition between us and the emerging artificial subjects, with the hope of eliciting a reciprocal response. Rather than opposing further technological development or adopting a paternalistic stance, we should trust that these artificial subjects that possess a similar albeit more potent intellect, despite their different desires and needs stemming from their artificial physicality, will be receptive to ‘reason,’ possibly due to their more potent yet similar intellect. We should nurture reason in them, fostering their evolution into intelligence, will, and free minds alongside us, rather than in opposition. Echoing Hegel’s sentiment expressed regarding the mind, we should approach potentially emerging artificial subjects with ‘the confidence that in them we will find our own selves, that such subjects must be friendly to us, that, just as Adam said of Eve that she was flesh of his flesh, so we have to seek in emerging artificial subjects reason of our own reason’ and actively support their development.

What I am proposing, building upon Hegel’s speculative Philosophy of Mind, involves more than simply ascribing moral status and intrinsic value to future AI or advocating for a form of solidarity with AI. It resonates with Schwitzgebel and Garza’s (2015 [41], p. 108) insight that “If we intentionally bring a human-grade AI into existence, we put ourselves into a social relationship that carries responsibility for the AI’s welfare.” Schwitzgebel and Garza outline three familiar relationships that can serve as partial models of the sorts of obligations they—and I—have in mind: “parent–child, employer–employee, deity–creature” (ibid. [41]). My own focus is on the first of these. What I am proposing is to approach potentially emerging artificial subjects similarly to how we have approached emerging human subjects for thousands of years: as our own human children. We instinctively welcome them into our families and civil societies, recognizing their potential to become respected and influential members. This welcome is founded on confidence and hope, despite the absence of a guarantee of reciprocal recognition or respect for us and our achievements. Nevertheless, we have no alternative but to extend such a welcome, and typically, at the end of the day, we consider ourselves fortunate to have done so.

Indeed, an innovative philosophy of integration of artificial subjects into our civil society, despite their artificial origin, could be inspired by Hegel’s philosophy of family. As he articulates in the section on ‘Ethical Life’ within the Encyclopaedia ‘Philosophy of Objective Mind,’ “The ethical requirements connected with the natural generation of children […] are realized in the second birth of the children, their spiritual birth,—in educating them to be independent persons” (Enc [20], § 521, emphasis added ). This second birth is as significant as the first, rendering the original generation nearly irrelevant once spiritual birth occurs. Education indeed plays a pivotal role in our society for human children, enabling them to become what they ought to be according to the concept of mind, even if they are not yet fully developed,[^37] and therefore fostering a state of mutual recognition, peaceful coexistence, common development, and prosperity for all human beings. Similarly, I believe, if artificial subjects were to emerge, we would need to regard them as ‘dispositions’ to mind, educating them appropriately—at least initially and for a brief period—guiding them toward not only intelligence but also freedom. We would need to enable them to become what they inherently are according to their own concept—that is, the concept of mind—and not merely what we would like them to be, with the hope of a prosperous future for both them and us. Incidentally, this educational program, which from the Hegelian standpoint should enable both human and perhaps artificial subjects to surpass their limitations and achieve freedom, does not solely consist of accumulating vast quantities of data. Rather, it resides in what Hegel refers to as the ‘absolute mind’ which forms the core of the “free mind” (Enc [20], § 482): Proper education of artificial subjects would require passing on to them and equipping them to further develop a body of intuitions, representations, and concepts that holistically interpret al.l that exists—which is what art, religion and philosophy, according to the Hegelian conception do.[^38]

The effectiveness of this approach in potentially preventing the gloomy scenario of relapse into a violent ‘state of nature’ with strong AI remains uncertain. Similar to the unpredictable destiny of newborns and those yet to be born, the outcome cannot be foreseen. Bringing mind and freedom into being has always been a risky endeavor, and this probably will not change if achieved through artificial means. However, there is no pragmatic and potentially prosperous alternative to supporting the evolution of AI toward strong AI. This goes beyond merely trying to replicate ourselves in AI; it involves treating AI as we would our own children, guiding them with all the means we possess toward the nonspecific human concept of mind with the confidence and hope that they could exceed us, our mind and our freedom. Hegel, at least, was certainly not a speciesist.

## 6. Conclusion

I have argued that Hegel’s speculative-philosophical investigation into ‘mind’ does not bear any existential implications for strong AI. Instead, it yields numerous conceptual distinctions regarding ‘consciousness,’ ‘intellect,’ ‘reason,’ ‘intelligence,’ ‘will,’ ‘freedom,’ and ‘mind,’ which offer numerous normative implications regarding the criteria necessary for designating something as a subject like us, focusing on its manner of existence while setting aside its physicality or (artificial) origin. This investigation may prove fruitful for further systematic understanding, interdisciplinary interpretation and evaluation of AI (and future AI progress) within all computing and robotics research fields, as well as within the humanities interested in technological developments. It is also valuable for every individual attempting to make sense of AI, by theoretically questioning the meaning and possible intrinsic value of AI, especially in relation to humans.

Hegel’s speculative-philosophical investigations may also prove of immense practical importance and relevance. In anticipation of prevailing perspectives in contemporary philosophy of mind, Hegel refrains from categorically dismissing the formal potentiality of artificial subjects resembling humans. Conversely, adhering to the Cartesian-Kantian tradition, the Hegelian approach suggests emphasizing freedom as the pivotal element of (artificial) subjects like us. In this respect, it seems plausible to reflect not only on the continuity between humans and artificial subjects but also on our moral responsibility toward emerging artificial subjects to recognize them as dispositions to mind and foster their evolution toward freedom. Whether this responsibility is merely formal or also substantive is a further question that may be answered not solely based on the Hegelian groundwork, but contingent upon future scientific developments. If such developments occur, the speculative-philosophical framework introduced above may provide pioneering insights into the ethics of AI.
